Sagittal MSM

Sagittal MSM, a faster approach to multi-scalar multiplication (MSM) of elliptic-curve points, one of the most expensive steps in zero-knowledge proving. This page explains it from the ground up, with step-by-step animations.

I invented the mathematical approach in 2022, built Python operation models and a C++ reference, then directed and reviewed the CPU and GPU implementations at Sindri Labs with colleagues and a Draper Labs contractor. The benchmark results below were a crucial part of the technical case behind Sindri's $5M seed round, led by CoinFund.

Source on GitHub Full docs 2023 write-up

On BLS12-377 at 220 points, with the proving key precomputed once and reused across proofs, Sagittal ran an MSM in 64 ms: 1.7× faster than every ZPrize 2022 GPU entry that runs on a V100, and faster than all entries but one (Yrrid, 48 ms) on an A10G.

1. MSM and elliptic-curve arithmetic

A zero-knowledge proof lets a prover convince a verifier that a computation was done correctly without revealing its inputs. Most proof systems in use (Groth16, PLONK, and anything built on KZG polynomial commitments) commit to large polynomials, and committing to a polynomial with n coefficients is one MSM of n points. That is why MSMs dominate proving time.

A dot product

A dot product multiplies two lists pairwise and adds up the results. A multi-scalar multiplication (MSM) has the same shape, except the second list holds elliptic-curve points instead of numbers:

Numbers: a dot product
(3, 5, 2) · (4, 1, 7)
= 3·4 + 5·1 + 2·7 = 31
3 multiplications, 2 additions
Points: a multi-scalar multiplication
(3, 5, 2) · (P1, P2, P3)
= 3·P1 + 5·P2 + 2·P3
Each k·P means P + P + … + P, k times

The scalars ki are ordinary integers. The points Pi are pairs (x, y) of numbers that satisfy a curve equation. In a zero-knowledge prover the points are fixed (they come from the proving key and are the same for every proof of a circuit) and the scalars change every proof. Anything computed from the points alone can be computed once and reused.

Adding two elliptic-curve points

The curves here have the equation y² = x³ + b for a constant b. Over the real numbers, adding two points has a picture: draw the line through P and Q, find where it meets the curve again, and reflect across the x-axis. Cryptography uses the same formulas with every number taken modulo a large prime p (shown below). There is also an identity point O (the "point at infinity"): P + O = P, and P + (−P) = O, where −P = (x, −y).

λ  = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁)      for doubling: λ = 3x₁² / (2y₁)
x₃ = λ² − x₁ − x₂
y₃ = λ(x₁ − x₃) − y₁

Modulo p, dividing means multiplying by an inverse, which costs hundreds of multiplications. So implementations carry a running denominator in extra coordinates and divide once at the end. A point stored as plain (x, y) is affine. Jacobian coordinates store (X, Y, Z) with x = X/Z² and y = Y/Z³; XYZZ coordinates store (X, Y, ZZ, ZZZ) with x = X/ZZ and y = Y/ZZZ, the form the fastest GPU MSMs use. One addition then costs about 10–16 field multiplications: 10 for a mixed add (a running sum + a stored affine point), 14 for a full add (two running sums) and 9 for a doubling, in XYZZ.

There is no multiply for elliptic-curve points

A curve has only addition. "k·P" means adding P to itself k times. There is no way to multiply a point by a number directly, or to multiply two points, and recovering k from k·P (the discrete logarithm) is believed infeasible. That is what makes these curves useful for cryptography.

A more efficient way to add

In binary, each digit is worth twice the digit to its right: 1, 2, 4, 8, … So 13 is 1101 in binary: 8 + 4 + 0 + 1. Reading a binary number from the left, each new digit doubles everything read so far, then adds itself: 1 = 1, 11 = 2·1 + 1 = 3, 110 = 2·3 = 6, 1101 = 2·6 + 1 = 13.

Without a multiply, 13·P means adding P to itself 12 times: P + P + … + P. Double-and-add follows the binary digits instead: start with P, then for each remaining digit double what you have (P + P is one addition) and add P if the digit is 1. That reaches 13·P in 5 additions instead of 12: 3 doublings and 2 additions of P. The gap grows fast: for a 20-bit k, about 30 additions instead of up to a million. Curves modulo a prime: finite fields and subgroups

Cryptography doesn't use real numbers. It takes every number modulo a prime p, so the coordinates are whole numbers from 0 to p − 1: a finite field. The addition formulas above still work, but the smooth curve turns into a scatter of about p dots. O isn't one of the dots; it's one extra point with no coordinates.

Provers don't use every dot. They start at one point G and keep adding G: G, 2G, 3G, … until the sequence reaches O. That loop is a subgroup, and its size is called r. After O the loop repeats, (r + 1)·G = G, like a clock with r hours, so a scalar k only matters mod r. Here it is on y² = x³ + 3 with p = 37, small enough to see:

r is prime on purpose: then every point in the loop except O leads around the whole loop, and an attacker can't split the problem into smaller pieces.

A real curve: BLS12-377

Everything on this page uses BLS12-377 (introduced in the Zexe paper), the curve of the ZPrize MSM competition. Its equation is y² = x³ + 1, and its two primes are far too big to draw:

  • p, 377 bits. Coordinates are numbers modulo p. In full, p is the 114-digit number 258664426012969094010652733694893533536393512754914660539884262666720468348340822774968888139573360124440321458177.
  • r, 253 bits. The subgroup provers use has r points, and scalars are numbers modulo r. In full, r is the 76-digit number 8444461749428370424248824938781546531375899335154063827935233455917409239041.

So a scalar has 253 bits, and double-and-add takes about 252 doublings and 126 additions: 376 curve operations per point, measured. A naive MSM does that for every point, about 400 million curve operations at 220 points. Where these sizes come from: Terms.

2. Big numbers and GPUs

Numbers bigger than a machine word

A machine word is the size of number a processor handles in one instruction: 64 bits on a CPU and 32 bits for a GPU's integer units. A 377-bit number is stored as 12 words of 32 bits, and multiplying two of them works like long multiplication on paper, one word pair at a time:

The product then has to be reduced mod p back to 12 words. Done directly, that is a long division, slower than the multiplication itself. Montgomery form avoids it: store each value a as a·R mod p, with R = 2384. Multiplying two stored values gives abR², and getting back to abR means dividing by R, which for a power of 2 just drops the low words. Values are converted in once at the start and out once at the end. Sindri's GPU code kept everything in Montgomery form.

The standard way to compute one Montgomery multiplication, CIOS (Coarsely Integrated Operand Scanning; Koç, Acar and Kaliski, 1996), interleaves multiplying and reducing word by word. It takes 2s² + s word multiply-adds for s words: 300 per field multiplication, and about 3,000 per curve addition.

The exact formulas the GPU kernel ran

Sagittal's GPU kernel kept its running sums (the buckets of sections 3 and 4) in Jacobian coordinates and its precomputed table entries affine, so each lookup is a mixed add. These are the exact formulas it ran, with every value in Montgomery form (above): each product or square is one Montgomery multiplication, and additions and small multiples are ordinary modular additions.

Mixed add: (X1, Y1, Z1) + (x2, y2)        7 multiplications + 4 squarings
  Z1Z1 = Z1²
  U2   = x2 · Z1Z1                        x2 scaled to the bucket's denominator
  S2   = y2 · Z1 · Z1Z1                   y2 scaled likewise
  H    = U2 − X1                          x-difference (λ's denominator)
  HH   = H²,   I = 4·HH,   J = H · I
  r    = 2 · (S2 − Y1)                    y-difference (λ's numerator)
  V    = X1 · I
  X3   = r² − J − 2·V
  Y3   = r · (V − X3) − 2 · Y1 · J
  Z3   = (Z1 + H)² − Z1Z1 − HH            = 2·Z1·H, the new denominator

Double: 2·(X1, Y1, Z1)                    2 multiplications + 5 squarings
  A = X1²,   B = Y1²,   C = B²
  D = 2 · ((X1 + B)² − A − C)             = 4·X1·B
  E = 3 · A,   F = E²                     3x² from the tangent slope 3x²/(2y)

GPU memory and threads

A GPU program is called a kernel (CUDA guide). It runs as thousands of threads, organized two ways: blocks of up to 1,024 threads that share a block of fast on-chip memory and can wait for each other, and warps of 32 threads that execute one instruction together. Threads in a warp that need to do different things wait for each other. Memory comes in tiers:

RegistersUp to 255 × 32-bit per thread. One XYZZ accumulator alone takes 48.
Shared memory48 KB per block by default, on-chip. Holds one Sagittal table for g ≤ 9.
Global memory16–48 GB, hundreds of cycles away. Points, scalars and precomputed tables.
Host memoryAcross PCIe. Where Sagittal combines its 253 buckets.

Why data-dependent work is costly on a GPU

In Pippenger's bucket method the bucket a point goes to depends on its scalar, so many threads can target the same bucket at once. A bucket update is a curve addition (about 3,000 multiply-adds), not one instruction, so the GPU's atomic add (a hardware instruction that lets many threads add to the same number safely) can't do it: threads must take turns, or the points must be sorted by bucket first. Fast implementations avoid the collisions by sorting points by bucket and balancing bucket sizes, and the ZPrize winners do that very well. But it is extra work every proof. An algorithm whose work is fixed in advance doesn't pay it.

3. Pippenger's bucket method

Every fast MSM builds on this (Bernstein on Pippenger). With n points, write each scalar in base 2c: c-bit digits called windows. In each window every point goes into the bucket for its digit. Then a running sum from the top bucket down computes Σ d·bucket[d] with about two additions per bucket, and windows are combined by doubling c times between them. Step through 8 scalars with c = 4:

The best window grows with n: c = 17 at 220 points, which means 15 windows × 65,536 buckets ≈ 983,000 buckets, filled by the data. (65,536 is 216, not 217: signed digits halve the buckets.) The ZPrize 2022 winners (Yrrid and MatterLabs) also precompute shifted copies of every point so all windows fold into a few bucket sets.

4. Sagittal MSM

The sagittal plane divides a body into left and right. Sagittal MSM divides the scalar bit matrix the other way from Pippenger. Pippenger cuts it into strips c bits wide and n rows tall; Sagittal cuts it into blocks 1 bit wide and g rows tall. A block's g bits select one subset sum of its batch's points. That sum depends only on the points, so every subset sum is precomputed once per proving key.

Step 1, once per proving key: build the tables

Each batch of g points gets its own table holding every combination (not permutation: P1 + P2 = P2 + P1) of its points: 2g − 1 entries, each built with one addition from two entries built earlier. Here, 8 points in batches of g = 4 need 2 tables:

Step 2, every proof: look up table entries and add them into buckets

For each batch and each bit, the g scalar bits form a pattern that picks one table entry, added to the bucket for that bit. There is one bucket per scalar bit. The bit patterns match the table above: 0101 is P2 + P4 in both.

Finally the b buckets (b = 253 scalar bits) are combined with Horner’s rule: start from the top bucket and repeat result = 2·result + bucket[i] down to bit 0, the same doubling chain as double-and-add with bucket i in place of P. That is b − 1 doublings and b − 1 additions in total, about 500 operations whatever n is. There is no bucket reduction, every bucket gets exactly one lookup per batch, and every addition is the cheap mixed kind.

Relation to Straus's method. Straus's method (also called Shamir's trick) computes k1·P1 + … + kg·Pg for a handful of points by precomputing all 2g subset sums, then running one double-and-add over all g scalars together. One Sagittal batch is exactly that. What Sagittal adds:

  • every batch runs at once, with one bucket per bit, so the doublings are done only once for all n points instead of once per batch;
  • the tables are built offline, once per fixed proving key;
  • every bucket's work is fixed before any scalar is read.

Cost formulas and measured counts for every batch size g: docs/4-sagittal.md.

5. Why Sagittal suits GPUs

At 220 points Sagittal does more curve operations than a tuned Pippenger. It still ran faster on GPUs in 2023, because of the shape of its work:

  • Thread = bit, block = batch. Every thread does one lookup and one addition per batch, so warps don't diverge.
  • Tables in shared memory. A batch's 2g entries of 96 bytes fit in 48 KB for g ≤ 9.
  • A fixed order. The fastest kernel spreads blocks over 256 copies of the buckets by block number, not by the data, and the 253 final buckets are combined on the CPU.

The catch is memory. Tables hold (2g − 1)/g points per input point, so large MSMs force small g. On a 16 GiB V100 the largest g that fits is 8 at 222 and 5 at 224, exactly what the 2023 runs used.

How the kernel maps batches to thread blocks and bits to threads, and why g stays at 9 or below: docs/5-gpu.md.

Against the Yrrid configuration on the same A10G, Sagittal does 1.87× more curve operations and 1.45× more field multiplications, but was only 1.33× slower (64 vs 48 ms). Field cost explains most of the gap; the shape of the work explains the rest.

Precompute is cheap. Sagittal's tables cost 385 field multiplications per point to build, about 5× less than the Yrrid configuration's shifted points (2,110). For a single proof, Sagittal costs 743 per point against 2,357; the Yrrid configuration pulls ahead only above 16 proofs per proving key.

7. How the numbers are made

The repository is a Python reference implementation that counts every operation. Algorithms only call group operations inside named phases, and the group counts them, so the counts always describe the code that ran. They run on BN254, BLS12-377 and a 64-bit Goldilocks test curve (in Jacobian or XYZZ coordinates, with field operations counted too), and on a fast counting group with the same structure. A closed-form model, tested against measured counts within 2%, extends the comparison to 224 points.

The animations above use small examples (8 points, 4–8-bit scalars) so every step fits on screen. Every number on this page comes from benchmarks/results/ or the 2023 GPU logs, and the methodology page lists what they don't cover.

Terms and references

Field, field element, field multiplication
The numbers 0 to p − 1, where adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing all wrap around modulo the prime p. A field element is one such number; a field multiplication multiplies two of them mod p. Curve coordinates are field elements.
p and r: why 377 and 253 bits
On BLS12-377 the curve has h·r points, about p of them (Hasse's theorem), where r is a 253-bit prime and h a 125-bit cofactor. Provers use the subgroup of r points because the discrete logarithm is only as hard as the largest prime factor of the group's order (Pohlig–Hellman). The sizes come from how BLS12 curves are built: from one 64-bit number x, r = x4 − x2 + 1 (253 bits) and p ≈ x6/3 (377 bits). BLS12-377's x was also chosen so that 247 divides r − 1, which the NTTs (FFTs over a finite field) inside provers need.
Identity point O, and −P
The curve's "zero": P + O = P. The negative of P = (x, y) is −P = (x, −y), and P + (−P) = O.
Circuit, proving key
A circuit is the computation being proved, written as arithmetic constraints. The proving key is the fixed set of curve points (from a one-time setup) that every proof of that circuit uses. Those points are the Pi of the MSM.
Affine, Jacobian, XYZZ
Ways to store a point. Affine is plain (x, y). Jacobian is (X, Y, Z) with x = X/Z², y = Y/Z³. XYZZ is (X, Y, ZZ, ZZZ) with x = X/ZZ, y = Y/ZZZ. The last two avoid division until the end.
Kernel, thread block, warp, atomic add
A kernel is a program that runs on the GPU. Its threads are grouped into blocks (up to 1,024 threads sharing fast on-chip memory) and warps (32 threads running one instruction together). An atomic add is a hardware instruction that lets many threads add to the same number without losing updates; it works on single machine numbers, not on curve points.
n, b, c, g
n is the number of points (and scalars) in the MSM. b is the number of scalar bits (253 for BLS12-377). c is Pippenger's window width in bits. g is Sagittal's batch size: points per precomputed table.
Sagittal plane
In anatomy, the vertical plane that divides a body into left and right halves (Wikipedia). Sagittal MSM is named for its slicing: it cuts the scalar bit matrix into columns one bit wide, where Pippenger cuts strips c bits wide.
Bucket, table entry
A bucket is a running sum of points. In Pippenger there is one per possible digit value per window; in Sagittal, one per scalar bit. A table entry is one precomputed subset sum of a batch's points.
Signed digits
Pippenger window digits from −(2c−1 − 1) to 2c−1 instead of 0 to 2c − 1. A negative digit adds −P, which is nearly free (flipping the sign of y is one field subtraction), so each window needs half as many buckets: 2c−1 instead of 2c.
Straus's method, Shamir's trick
Two names for computing k1·P1 + … + kg·Pg by precomputing all 2g subset sums and running one double-and-add over all g scalars together. Straus gave it as the solution to Problem 5125 (Bellman and Straus, American Mathematical Monthly, 1964); the same idea appears in ElGamal's 1985 signature paper. Textbook treatment: Handbook of Applied Cryptography, §14.6.
Yrrid configuration
This project's model of the parameters Yrrid used in its winning ZPrize 2022 entry: window c = 23, signed digits, and shifted point copies that fold all windows into 2 bucket sets. It is a model of their algorithm's operation counts, not their code.
ZPrize
A 2022 competition to accelerate zero-knowledge proving. Its GPU MSM track (BLS12-377, batches of 226 points) was led by Yrrid and MatterLabs, whose entries were later combined into one (challenge, results, entries).
BLS12-377, BN254, Goldilocks
BLS12-377 (introduced in the Zexe paper) and BN254 are curves widely used in zero-knowledge proofs (377- and 254-bit fields; 253- and 254-bit scalars). Goldilocks is the prime 264 − 232 + 1, popular because its numbers fit in a 64-bit word (used by Plonky2); this project uses a small test curve over it. BN254 is the curve behind Ethereum's pairing precompiles (EIP-197).
V100, A10G, Ampere, sppark
The V100 (2017, Volta generation, 16 GB) and A10G (Ampere generation, 24 GB) are NVIDIA GPUs. Ampere is the 2020 generation; Yrrid's code requires it. sppark is Supranational's open-source GPU library for MSMs and field arithmetic (GitHub); Sindri's kernel used its field and point types.
